01 / THE PROBLEM

THE SCREEN
STAYS THE SAME.
People don't.

The same screen presentation does not work equally well for everyone. The project begins with the everyday adjustments people already make—and asks how those choices might become easier to explore and control.

The problem

People who experience visual difficulties while using digital screens may rely on different workarounds, such as adjusting brightness, changing text size, switching display modes, taking breaks or using audio. These approaches vary by person and task, and users may need to keep experimenting to find what works for them.

Research Question

How might a browser-based interface help people with visual difficulties discover and reuse screen-presentation adjustments, without assuming one setting works for everyone?

Design response

Make changes easier to evaluate and reverse. VisionEase pairs guided support with direct controls, then separates Draft, Preview, Apply and Undo. Saved views let people return to a configuration within the prototype.

Layer 1: Raw Evidence

I used semi-structured interviews and qualitative synthesis to understand reported screen-use difficulties and the workarounds people already use. These are qualitative observations, not evidence of clinical outcomes.

Layer 2: Synthesis

The documented workarounds included adjusting brightness, using dark mode, relying on third-party tools, taking breaks, and substituting audio. Notably, a younger pupil (outside the target demographic) reported that zoomed magnification introduced double vision issues.

Design implication

A single “best” setting would be an assumption. The interface should support personal choice, comparison and reversibility.

Layer 3: Evidence to Insight

After grouping the interview accounts into recurring themes, I examined what they might suggest about people's screen-use experiences and translated those interpretations into design directions to explore.

Evidence
Interpretation
Design Proposal
A participant described repeatedly adjusting screen brightness.
Finding a suitable display setting may involve repeated manual adjustment.
Allow users to compare adjustments before committing to a preferred configuration.
Participants reported that small or dense text, such as in case studies or exams, disrupted reading.
Text scaling needs vary by task and context, rather than being a single fixed requirement.
Provide easily accessible typography controls grouped by reading comfort.
Participants described using multiple workarounds, including display adjustments, breaks and audio.
We hypothesize that manually re-establishing a comfortable setup for each new task is highly repetitive and interrupts focus.
Allow users to save and quickly reapply task-labelled configurations to minimize repeated setup.
A school pupil (outside the target age demographic) reported that close or zoomed viewing sometimes introduced double vision.
While this is a contextual observation rather than proof for the primary adult audience, it highlights that universal "accessibility" fixes like magnification can introduce conflicting issues.
Make all adjustments optional and highly reversible, rather than forcing a universal default.

04 / From evidence to interaction

Make control
feel tangible.

Two decisions shaped the interaction: start with the user’s difficulty, and make every proposed adjustment easy to review before it is applied.

01
Entry point

Start with the difficulty, not the setting name

Earlier direction

Technical controls such as contrast and saturation asked users to know which setting might help.

Design response

Ask Anna begins with a plain-language description, while Adjust myself remains available for people who prefer direct control.

02
Control and reversibility

See the change before committing.

Earlier direction

Immediate changes blurred the difference between a draft and a setting that had been applied.

Design response

A clear Draft → Preview → Apply / Undo sequence creates a decision point before committing a change. Saved views support reuse within the prototype.

These changes reflect design critique and iteration. Independent usability testing is still pending.

08 / Reflection

Not one ideal.
More room for choice.

What is demonstrated

Reversible interaction states

The browser prototype and browser checks cover the main adjustment route, preview behavior, Apply and Undo, and saved configurations within VisionEase.

What remains unverified

Usability and clinical outcomes

Independent participant usability testing has not yet been completed. The project does not establish clinical relief, improved vision or a universally comfortable palette.

Next evaluation

Can people complete the adjustment flow without help?

I would ask participants to find a relevant adjustment, preview it, apply it, undo it and save a view. I would observe whether they can complete each step independently and where the interaction creates confusion.

Key learning

I began by thinking in terms of finding a “best” setting. The research encouraged a different framing: support people in exploring options, seeing the effect within the prototype, and retaining control over whether to keep a change.
